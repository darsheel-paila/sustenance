Why cravings matter

Cravings are signals—about emotional needs, comfort, or what your body might be asking for. Honoring them with curiosity (instead of guilt) is a powerful step toward gentle nourishment.

Gentle nutrition

We believe in working with your cravings, not against them. Small, sustainable choices that feel good in your body matter more than perfection.

Small wins

Every positive step counts. One satisfying, nourishing choice is a win. We're here to celebrate progress with you—no judgment, just support.

Common nutrition misconceptions

These are broad patterns supported by high-quality research (systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and randomized trials). Individual needs vary—especially for medical conditions, eating disorder recovery, and athletic training.

“Carbs are bad.” ⌄

What research tends to show: outcomes depend more on carb quality (fiber-rich, minimally processed) and the overall diet pattern than carbs as a category.

Practical take: if you want steadier energy or appetite, try pairing carbs with protein + fiber + fat rather than eliminating them.

“Fat makes you fat.” ⌄

What research tends to show: fats are essential for hormones, absorption of vitamins (A, D, E, K), and satisfaction. Health effects depend on type of fat and the food context.

Practical take: prioritize fats from foods like nuts, seeds, olive oil, avocado, fish, and use them to make meals satisfying.

“Protein is only for bodybuilders.” ⌄

What research tends to show: higher-protein patterns can support satiety and help preserve lean mass during weight loss—especially when combined with resistance training.

Practical take: aim for a protein “anchor” at meals (yogurt, eggs, beans, tofu, chicken, fish) and build around it.

“Calories are all that matters / calories don’t matter at all.” ⌄

What research tends to show: energy balance matters, but food form (liquid vs solid), protein/fiber, and ultra-processing can change hunger and spontaneous intake.

Practical take: if you want “easier” nutrition, focus on protein + fiber + minimally processed meals first; calorie targets are a tool, not a moral score.

FAQ

Quick, supportive answers. Bodies vary, and medical conditions + eating disorder recovery can change what “normal” looks like—when in doubt, talk with a qualified clinician.

Will eating 2000 calories a day make me gain weight? ⌄

Not necessarily. Weight change depends on your energy needs (age, sex, height, weight, muscle mass, activity, sleep, stress, medications, and more). For some people, 2000 is a deficit; for others, it’s maintenance or a surplus.

A helpful way to think about it: if, over time, intake is above what your body uses, weight tends to trend up; if below, it tends to trend down. Day-to-day scale changes also reflect water, glycogen, sodium, and digestion—not just fat gain.

If your goal is stability: aim for consistent meals, adequate protein + fiber, and watch the trend over a few weeks (not single days).

Why am I hungrier when I “eat healthy”? ⌄

Sometimes “healthy” meals end up being lower calorie and lower protein than you expect (e.g., mostly produce). Hunger can be a sign you need more protein, carbs, or fats—not that you’re doing it wrong.

Try this: keep the veggies, but add a protein anchor (yogurt/eggs/beans/tofu/chicken) and a satisfying carb or fat (rice/oats/bread/olive oil/nuts).

Is it “bad” to eat late at night? ⌄

Timing matters less than total intake, food choices, and sleep. Eating late isn’t inherently harmful—sometimes it’s necessary (shift work, workouts, long days).

If late-night eating feels chaotic, it can help to eat enough earlier and keep a planned, satisfying option at night (e.g., yogurt + fruit, toast + eggs, oatmeal).
